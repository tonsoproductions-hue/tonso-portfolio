Before we started

When I came on, every single operational task was done by hand. The founder was the system — holding it all in her head, her inbox, and a tangle of spreadsheets that only she could decode. Crucially, there was no defined client journey at all. Leads came in and were handled differently every time, with no consistent process from first contact through to event day.

Phase 1 — The client journey we built

A core deliverable was designing and implementing a seven-stage client pipeline in HoneyBook — the first structured customer journey this business had ever had. Each stage has defined triggers, automated actions, and clear handoff criteria so nothing gets lost and no client experiences an inconsistent process.

What I built in Phase 1

  • Seven-stage client pipeline — Designed and implemented the brand's first structured customer journey in HoneyBook, from initial onboarding through to event day, with defined triggers and automated handoffs at every stage.
  • HoneyBook CRM configuration — Custom pipelines, intake questionnaires, proposal templates, contracts, retainer invoices, and escrow-style trial payment flows tailored to the brand's booking model.
  • Google Sheets contractor system — A structured tracker covering onboarding status, agreed rates, project assignments, deliverables, and payout history — giving every contractor an auditable record for the first time.
  • Zapier & Make.com automation layer — Multi-step automations connecting HoneyBook pipeline events to the Sheets tracker automatically, eliminating manual data entry across both systems.
  • Google Maps API integration — Location-aware logic at the event checklist stage, replacing manual proximity lookups with automated contractor matching.
  • SOPs and handoff documentation — Lightweight operating guides so the founder could maintain and extend the system independently after handoff.

Phase 2 — Outgrowing the spreadsheet

The first build worked, and the business grew into it. By mid-2026 one Google Sheet had quietly become the operating system for the entire business: booked jobs arriving from HoneyBook, artist assignments, per-artist rate cards, a payout calculation running across forty-plus columns, and a scripted contractor finder ranking artists by drive time. I audited it end to end and wrote up where the architecture was straining before proposing anything new.

Fragile

Records matched by name

With no real row identity, a client who booked twice could point at the wrong job, and re-runs appended duplicate rows.

Fragile

Payout logic outgrew the grid

Nine interacting payout rules lived in spilled formulas. Typing in the wrong cell broke a column, usually noticed after a payout was already wrong.

Fragile

Services stored as columns

Adding a service meant a new column plus changes to the rate grid, the payout formula and every artist tab.

Fragile

No view per artist

One job exploded into many rows, and sharing an artist's tab would expose every other artist's rates.

A phased plan the owner could stop after any step

I wrote a statement of work for a three-phase migration to Airtable, sized in hours and weeks, with a sign-off checkpoint between phases, an explicit list of limitations, and a rule that every account sits in the client's name so there is no dependency on me to keep it running.

The data model

The most important change was structural: services became records instead of columns. Every planned service on a job is its own row, booked to one artist at that artist's rate for the event year, so adding an eighteenth service is data entry, not a rebuild, and changing a rate next year never rewrites last year's payouts.

Inquiry→ Client + Project→ Planned services→ Artist assignment→ Line items + travel legs→ Payout

Thirteen linked tables in total, including rates by artist and year, a distance matrix, and global settings for fees.

The automation layer

  • HoneyBook intake — A Zapier zap lands each new HoneyBook project in an inquiries table. An automation finds or creates the client by email, creates or reuses the project by HoneyBook ID, and flips it to Booked when the client books, without ever overwriting what is already there.
  • Drive-time matching — When a venue address is entered or changed, one Google Distance Matrix call measures driving miles from every active artist, fills a ranked shortlist for that job, and skips placeholder addresses like "Studio" or "TBD".
  • One-click staffing — Picking an artist on a planned service finds or creates her assignment, looks up her rate for that service and year, and creates the payout line item. If anything is missing it leaves a plain-language warning instead of a wrong number.
  • Travel pay that handles real weddings — Travel is built as one leg per address an artist covers, so a separate getting-ready location is paid correctly. A minimum travel fee, studio fees and early-bird fees come from one settings record.
  • Address-change recalculation — When an artist moves, one checkbox re-measures her miles on upcoming jobs, leaves anything already fully paid untouched, and writes a dated log of what changed.
  • Split payments, tracked — Each assignment carries a payout percentage, first and second payments with dates and paid flags, adjustments with a reason, and a live amount owed.

A design rule runs through all of it: automations only write inputs, never a payout or revenue number. Every dollar figure is a formula the owner can inspect, which is what makes the system trustworthy enough to pay people from.

Interfaces and migration

The owner works from two Airtable interfaces: a staffing dashboard showing booked jobs, services still unstaffed, revenue, artist payouts, margin, and unpaid balances by artist, and a custom "assign and pay" desk. In October 2026 I migrated the sheet's full history into the new base, keeping each record's original sheet ID so every payout can be traced back and reconciled.

My role

I lead both the strategic recommendations and the complete technical build-out, and I've stayed on as the business's operations partner as it grew. In Phase 1 I designed the seven-stage client pipeline from scratch, configured HoneyBook with custom templates and automated triggers, built the Google Sheets contractor system, and wired it together with Zapier, Make.com and the Google Maps API. In Phase 2 I audited that system, wrote the redesign brief and phased statement of work, designed the Airtable data model, wrote the scripted automations, built the owner's interfaces, and migrated the historical data.

Outcome

What had been a fully manual, process-free operation became a structured, largely automated one. Every client now moves through the same consistent journey, from first inquiry to event day, with automated touchpoints, payment milestones, and contractor assignments happening in the background. Payout disputes dropped to zero in the first billing cycle and the founder stopped spending her evenings reconciling records by hand. Phase 2 then replaced the spreadsheet that had become the business's single point of failure with a linked system where a booking, its artists, their travel and their pay all follow real relationships instead of cell references.
